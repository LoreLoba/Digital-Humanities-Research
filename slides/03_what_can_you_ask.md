# What Can You Ask of a Table?

*Three kinds of question, and the difference between them*

One of six short pre-workshop guides. No previous data or coding experience is assumed.

---

## Descriptive, analytical, interpretive

*SECTION 1*

- **Descriptive** — what is in the data? 'How many sites are in Kenya?'
- **Analytical** — is there a pattern? 'Do natural sites get listed in danger more?'
- **Interpretive** — what does it mean? 'Why might that be?'
- Each needs a different method and licenses a different claim
- The commonest error is answering the first and reporting the third

> This is the frame for step 1 of the assignment. A descriptive question has an arithmetic answer and cannot be wrong about causes because it makes no claim about them. An analytical question compares and needs some account of whether the difference could be chance. An interpretive question is where humanities training does its work — but it must rest on the first two, not skip them.

## A good question names the columns

*SECTION 1.1*

- Vague: 'What does this data say about African cinema?'
- Better: 'Which countries have the most films recorded, and in which decades?'
- The better version names `country` and `year` — you can start immediately
- If you cannot name the columns, the question is not yet answerable
- This is a test you can apply to your own question in ten seconds

> Encourage them to run this test on every question they write. It converts an intimidating blank page into a mechanical check. It also surfaces, very early, when a question needs data the table does not contain — which is the subject of deck six.

## The 'so what' test

*SECTION 2*

- A finding is interesting when someone could act differently because of it
- Or when it contradicts what people assume
- 'Most African migrants move within Africa' passes — it contradicts the assumption
- 'The dataset has 8,037 rows' does not pass — it is a fact about the file
- Aim your question at something a reader would be surprised by

> The migration example is from the course itself and is worth quoting: just over half of African migrants live in another African country, which contradicts most public discussion. That is what a finding looks like. Encourage groups to guess the answer before computing it — if the guess turns out right, that is a weak finding; if it turns out wrong, they have something.

## Questions this data cannot answer

*SECTION 3*

- Anything about causes — data shows patterns, not reasons
- Anything about time, if you only have one point in time
- Anything about people the data never recorded
- Saying so is a finding, not a failure
- Your codebook lists these limits explicitly — read them first

> Six of seven of you said you are nervous. Here is the reassuring part: naming what the data cannot support is a large part of the mark, and it is a humanities skill rather than a technical one. A group that says clearly 'we cannot answer this and here is why' does better than one that produces a confident number that does not hold.

## Try it before Wednesday

*SECTION 4*

- Write one descriptive and one analytical question about your dataset
- Underline the column names in each
- If you cannot underline any, rewrite the question
- Write one question your dataset definitely cannot answer
- Bring all three

> The third one is the most valuable and the easiest to write, which is why it is included — it gives an anxious group an immediate win.
